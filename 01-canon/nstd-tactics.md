# NSTD (Voss) Tactics Canon

Owner: Brand voice panel. Status: canonical reference. Version: nstd-canon-v1.0.0. Last updated: 2026-05-19.

Compressed reference for the twenty-one Chris Voss / Never Split the Difference tactics applied to premium DTC funnel design. Each tactic covers the canon reference, a one-line definition, the funnel stages where it lands hardest, and the places where it should NOT be used. The "where to use" guidance is pattern-level - adapt the implementation to your brand's actual touchpoints and voice register.

## The thesis in one paragraph

Every funnel step is a micro-negotiation. The capture popup negotiates an email address against a perceived 30 seconds of attention. The first email negotiates an opening of the inbox against the reader's instinct to swipe-archive. The cart page negotiates the price against the social risk of becoming the kind of person who buys this category at this price online. Each gate has a different counterpart-state and a different leverage profile, but all of them respond to the same underlying mechanism. Label the emotion that is actually in the room. Then ask a calibrated question that hands the reader control.

## The twenty-one tactics

### 1. Tactical Empathy

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 3. The foundational concept that runs through every other tactic.

**One-line definition:** Deliberately understanding the counterpart's feelings in the moment, including what is behind them, and verbalising it back. Not sympathy, not agreement.

**Where to use:** Capture popup hero ("It seems like you are mid-scroll and not in the mood for a popup."). Problem-naming email opener (the lived-experience label). Price-objection email opener (the deeper "burned before" frame, not just the price reaction). Cart abandonment SMS first touch ("It looks like something pulled you away mid-checkout").

**Where NOT to use:** Transactional emails. Order confirmations and operational notifications should lead with operational fact, not empathy.

### 2. Mirroring

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 2. Repeating the last 1-3 words with upward inflection.

**One-line definition:** Repeat the most loaded 1-3 words of what they just said, with question intonation, then shut up.

**Where to use:** SMS reply auto-handler when the customer offers an objection. DM thread off-topic replies. Channel-specific reply protocols when the user replies with anything unexpected.

**Where NOT to use:** Broadcast emails and SMS. Mirroring requires the user to have said something first.

### 3. Labelling

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 3. "It seems like... / It sounds like... / It looks like..." openers.

**One-line definition:** Verbally name the emotion or dynamic you observe in your counterpart, always starting with "It seems / sounds / looks like".

**Where to use:** Email opening sentences (60-75% of welcome flow emails should open with a label per the Voss email adapter). Subject lines (label-as-subject pattern). Cart abandonment SMS second touch ("It looks like the second-thought after almost-clicking is usually...").

**Where NOT to use:** Operational confirmations. A transactional code delivery does not need a label.

### 4. Accusation Audit

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 3 and MasterClass.

**One-line definition:** List every negative thing the counterpart could possibly think about you, name them out loud before they raise them.

**Where to use:** Founder-story email opener (stacked 3-5 audits of the most likely customer suspicions - no credential, no celebrity ambassador, no traditional pedigree, etc.). Price-objection email (compound audit naming the three real objections - previous bad experience, status-quo alternative, partner-veto). Paid lander advertorial opener.

**Where NOT to use:** Single-line audits. The Voss-canonical pattern is 3-5 negatives stacked in one breath. One-line audits read soft.

### 5. No-Oriented Questions

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 4. "Yes is a commitment; no is a safeguard."

**One-line definition:** People feel safer saying no than yes. Reframe yes-questions into no-questions.

**Where to use:** Urgency subject line ("Should I let your discount expire tonight?" beats "Your discount ends tonight"). "Magic email" subject ("Have you decided this is not for you?"). Cart abandonment SMS final touch ("Would it be ridiculous to grab the box before the code releases at midnight?"). Capture popup CTA ("Any reason not to [try it]?"). Opt-down line ("Would you object to hearing from us only once a quarter?").

**Where NOT to use:** Yes-oriented questions and CTAs are banned per `07-anti-patterns/`. They trigger commitment-fatigue refusal.

### 6. "That's Right" Elicitation

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 5.

**One-line definition:** Build to "that's right" via a summary that paraphrases the counterpart's position plus labels their emotions, in your words.

**Where to use:** Future-pacing email (a seven-day visualisation that summarises the customer's lived week). Price-objection email closer ("Let me see if I have the picture right..."). Problem-naming email close ("Let me know if I have read this wrong - is the problem actually [X], or is it something else?").

**Where NOT to use:** Performative "that's right" without inviting correction. The summary must include a no-oriented confirmation question or it is brand-monologue, not negotiation.

### 7. Calibrated Questions (What/How)

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 7. "Successful negotiation involves getting your counterpart to suggest your solution himself."

**One-line definition:** Open-ended "How" / "What" questions that force the counterpart to think and that put a problem on their side of the table.

**Where to use:** Problem-naming email close ("What is the [thing] you are most [frustrated / tired / stuck] with?"). Onboarding email close ("How does this [plan] look against what you are already doing this week?"). Price-objection email close ("How would your [domain] change if [the underlying problem] actually got solved?"). DM thread ("What were you hoping to find out before you ordered?").

**Where NOT to use:** "Why" as an interrogative is banned per `07-anti-patterns/`. "Why did you sign up?" triggers defensiveness. Replace with "What brought you here?"

### 8. The Rule of Three

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 8.

**One-line definition:** Get the counterpart to agree to the same thing three times, in three different ways, in the same conversation. Counters counterfeit yeses.

**Where to use:** 3-stage confirmation arc across three separate emails on the same underlying commitment. Post-purchase order confirmation third-leg ("How would you like us to remind you when you are halfway through?").

**Where NOT to use:** Within a single email. Rule of Three operates across the welcome sequence, not within one send.

### 9. Bending Reality (Loss Aversion Framing)

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 6. Loss-framed scarcity outperforms gain-framed scarcity by approximately 2x.

**One-line definition:** People weight losses ~2x more than equivalent gains. Anchor on loss before talking gains.

**Where to use:** Onboarding email with a paired loss-frame ("The week with vs the week without"). Subscription pricing copy reframed as a recurring loss avoided ("Every box ordered one-time is [the per-unit gap] the brand cannot give back to you"). Cart abandonment SMS with two loss axes (code expiry + bonus release).

**Where NOT to use:** Discount-loss as the only loss frame. Voss-deeper version names two or three loss axes (financial, status, time, identity, opportunity).

### 10. Anchoring

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapters 6 and 9. Anchor with ranges, odd non-round numbers, and constraints.

**One-line definition:** The first number on the table sets the reference for everything that follows. Use ranges over points in writing.

**Where to use:** Price-objection email market-range anchor ("Premium [category] typically lands between [low] and [high] per month for a household of [size]"). PDP and checkout market-range anchor above the first-box price.

**Where NOT to use:** Single-point anchors when a range is available. Voss-canonical anchors are ranges; ship a range whenever the data supports one.

### 11. The F-Word: "Fairness"

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 6.

**One-line definition:** The word "fair" is the most emotionally loaded word in negotiation. Never use it reactively; only proactively. Substitute "real" / "honest" / "straight" in brand voice.

**Where to use:** Proactive deployment in the founder email ("If at any point any of this stops feeling real to you - the price, the claim, the founder story - tell me. I will not argue."). Graceful exit close ("When the time is right, it will feel real. If it never does, that is a real answer too.").

**Where NOT to use:** The word "fair" itself is banned per `07-anti-patterns/`. Reactive use ("we offer a fair price") triggers the negotiator's reflex Voss documents at length.

### 12. Time as Leverage / Deadlines

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 6. Deadlines are mostly invented. Never reveal yours.

**One-line definition:** Use deadlines structurally (system / supply) rather than personally (we / I need). Never bluff a deadline that is not real.

**Where to use:** Real production batch deadline ("There is one other deadline. The current batch finishes in [N weeks]. After that, the next run is [date]."). Cart abandonment SMS final touch with real supply deadline (only if supply is genuinely tight).

**Where NOT to use:** Time-only urgency with manufactured deadlines. "Sale ends at midnight" when the sale will run tomorrow. Exposed under consumer-protection law in most jurisdictions. The code expiry must actually fire when stated, or remove the expiry language.

### 13. The Late-Night FM DJ Voice

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 2. Calm, slow, downward inflection. In writing: short sentences, plain words, periods.

**One-line definition:** Calm, low, authoritative. Reads in writing as short sentences with period-terminated rhythm.

**Where to use:** Transactional emails. Urgency emails (DJ voice prevents urgency from reading as sales-panic). All subject lines.

**Where NOT to use:** Tonal drift into hype phrases or exclamations. Hype registers as panic. DJ voice cuts hype to numbers.

### 14. The 7-38-55 Rule (Writing Limit)

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 8. 7% words, 38% tone, 55% body language.

**One-line definition:** In writing, you lose 93% of the channel. Compensate with rhythm, line breaks, paragraph length, label placement. Watch for written analogues of incongruence: reply latency, length shift, pronoun shift.

**Where to use:** Reply protocol detection rule in DM and SMS. Short reply at long latency triggers calibrated-question follow-up, not sales-close.

**Where NOT to use:** As a content rule. 7-38-55 is a detection meta-principle, not a copy template.

### 15. The Pinocchio Effect

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 8.

**One-line definition:** Liars use more words, complex sentences, third-person pronouns, and stack disclaimers.

**Where to use:** As a self-audit. The brand's own copy can fail Pinocchio-tests if it overcompensates. Avoid hedges ("to be honest", "frankly", "to be clear") - they read as Pinocchio-stack indicators.

**Where NOT to use:** As an offensive tactic. The brand does not deploy Pinocchio detection against customers in any way the customer would notice.

### 16. The Pronoun Test

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 8. More "I" = less power; more "we/they" = more power and influence.

**One-line definition:** Decision-makers hide their authority behind a collective; gatekeepers perform authority with "I".

**Where to use:** First-person singular ("I") for the founder voice in founder-story emails, price-objection closer, urgency closer, sign-off, and P.S. lines. First-person plural ("we") for the brand collective in supply-chain claims, lab-testing claims, operational language.

**Where NOT to use:** Bureaucratic "we" or "the team" where the founder's voice would carry more authority. At early stage the founder anchor is usually stronger than the brand collective.

### 17. The Bargaining Style Triad

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 9. Analyst / Accommodator / Assertive.

**One-line definition:** Three negotiator personality types. Adapt or fail.

**Where to use:** Day-1 welcome email variant routing. Analyst variant leads with lab / ingredient / spec credentials. Accommodator variant leads with founder-story content abbreviated, family/community framing. Assertive variant leads with price-objection content abbreviated, hard maths.

**Where NOT to use:** A single welcome email to all signups. The Voss-coded version routes by signal (CTA clicked on landing, dwell-time, scroll depth, time of day). Even without a quiz, the first-touch signal is enough for a coarse Triad-route.

### 18. The Ackerman Method

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 9.

**One-line definition:** Four-step bargaining sequence with decreasing increments. Used when you are the one offering.

**Where to use:** Cart-abandonment cascade restructured as non-monetary-kicker Ackerman. Touch 1 holds the code with a label. Touch 2 calibrated question, no new offer. Touch 3 non-monetary kicker (a bonus item as throw-in, not extra discount). Touch 4 Magic Email no-oriented closer.

**Where NOT to use:** Stacking additional monetary discounts in cart-abandonment. The Ackerman version protects price integrity via non-monetary throw-ins.

### 19. Black Swans

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 10.

**One-line definition:** Hidden information that, if surfaced, transforms the negotiation. Every deal has at least three.

**Where to use:** Problem-naming email close Black Swan question ("What would have to be true for [the brand's offer] to be an easy yes for you?"). Post-purchase T+24h Black Swan ("What is the thing about [the domain] that we have not addressed?").

**Where NOT to use:** Pre-signup. Black Swan questions assume relationship; cold visitors do not yet have the trust budget to answer.

### 20. The Three Types of Leverage

**Canon reference:** Voss, NSTD Chapter 10. Positive (give), Negative (cost-label), Normative (their own words).

**One-line definition:** Three categories of leverage. Normative is the most underused in writing and the most powerful.

**Where to use:** Build a signup-intent capture in the popup ("Which of these brought you here?"). Quote it back in the urgency email ("You told us [their answer] is what brought you here."). Quarterly "you said" check-in for retention. Paid lander cultural-norm normative leverage (a true statement about long-standing behaviour in the audience's category).

**Where NOT to use:** Without the customer's prior statement. Normative leverage requires the customer to have stated something the brand can hold them to.

### 21. Voss's Magic Email

**Canon reference:** Voss canon - directly published by Voss on LinkedIn and the Black Swan blog.

**One-line definition:** Subject and body are the same no-oriented question. "Have you given up on this project?"

**Where to use:** T+30 days post-welcome-flow win-back ("Have you decided [the brand] is not for you?"). T+90 days quarterly re-engagement ("Have you moved on from this?").

**Where NOT to use:** More than once per customer per quarter. The Magic Email loses power if overused. Voss documents this caution explicitly.

## Supplementary tactics flagged from the Negotiation 9

**Dynamic Silence.** Voss specifies a 4+ second pause after labels and mirrors. In writing, the analogue is reply pacing. The brand that replies in 90 seconds reads as desperate. The brand that replies in 4-6 hours reads as deliberate. Voss-coded rule: never reply within 90 minutes of receipt for substantive customer messages; aim for the 4-24 hour window.

**Encouragers.** "Mm-hmm", "Go on", "Tell me more" - signals that the brand is listening. In writing: "Keep going." / "What else?" / "Tell me more about that." When a customer replies with an objection or a question, the encouragement-line opener doubles reply rates.
