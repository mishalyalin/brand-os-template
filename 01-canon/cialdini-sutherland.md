# Cialdini and Sutherland Canon

Owner: Brand voice panel. Status: canonical reference. Version: cialdini-sutherland-canon-v1.0.0. Last updated: 2026-05-19.

Compressed reference for the seven Cialdini principles and seven Sutherland moves applied to premium DTC funnel design, plus Schwartz's awareness-stages lens. Each principle covers the canon reference, a one-line definition, the funnel stages where it lands hardest, and the places where it should NOT be used. The "where to use" guidance is pattern-level: adapt the implementation to your brand's actual touchpoints, product, and category constraints.

## The thesis in one paragraph

Three frames sit beneath every successful premium DTC funnel. Cialdini's "click whirr" treats consumer decisions as fixed-action patterns, not deliberation. Sutherland's "context creates value" treats the unboxing experience, founder video, and editorial typography as costly signals that justify the premium price. Eugene Schwartz's awareness-stages model dictates that copy must match the prospect's current awareness stage, not the stage the brand wishes the prospect were at. Most new premium brands sit at Schwartz Stage 2-3 (problem-aware to solution-aware), dressed in editorial restraint, with Sutherland-grade costly signals, waiting for Cialdini's consensus trigger to load over the first six to twelve months of verified reviews.

## Cialdini's seven principles

### 1. Reciprocity

**Canon reference:** Cialdini, _Influence_ (1984), Chapter 2 "Reciprocation: The Old Give and Take".

**One-line definition:** Humans feel obliged to repay what another person has provided. Pre-emptive giving creates non-symmetric obligation.

**Where to use:** Move the most expensive reciprocation gift from email capture to first-order cart (e.g. a bonus item attached to the paid order, not the signup). Itemise unlock-able bonuses at retail value with strikethrough. Asymmetric founder-video DM or personalised note is the strongest reciprocation lever - personalised, unexpected gifts trigger reciprocation roughly 3x more strongly than equivalent-cost generic gifts per the Hare Krishna flower study.

**Where NOT to use:** Discount as the primary reciprocation lever. A signup percent-off code is the weakest reciprocation form - it reads as a vendor offer, not a gift.

### 2. Commitment and Consistency

**Canon reference:** Cialdini, _Influence_, Chapter 3 "Commitment and Consistency: Hobgoblins of the Mind".

**One-line definition:** Once a person has taken a small, public, or written action, they will work to remain consistent with it - and will accept a much larger downstream request that follows the consistency line.

**Where to use:** Single zero-stakes commitment between popup reveal and email field (one yes/no question with a single "Yes" button, written in the customer's lived language). Social-frame on subscription commit ("Most subscribers start with [tier]. Save X%."). Standard four-clause trust line covering pause / cancel / guarantee / regulatory credential.

**Where NOT to use:** Multi-step commitments that introduce friction at the popup. One micro-commitment is the right ceiling; three is the wrong floor.

### 3. Social Proof

**Canon reference:** Cialdini, _Influence_, Chapter 4 "Social Proof: Truths Are Us".

**One-line definition:** People decide what to do by watching what similar others do. Strength of effect rises with similarity, uncertainty, and quantity.

**Where to use:** Do not fake social proof - replace with structural credentials until reviews arrive. Three-stat trust band combining a lab/certification credential, a regulatory credential, and a transparency credential. Waitlist count framed as similar-others not total count (only ship the number if accurate). Review widget on PDP from day one even at zero reviews - the empty state is honest.

**Where NOT to use:** Fabricated review counts or customer counts. Cialdini Chapter 4 explicit: fabricated consensus collapses customer trust on first contradiction. Pre-launch counts are too small a denominator to credibly support a large round-number "customers" line.

### 4. Liking

**Canon reference:** Cialdini, _Influence_, Chapter 5 "Liking: The Friendly Thief".

**One-line definition:** We comply with requests from people we like. Liking rises from physical attractiveness, similarity, compliments, association, and contact-and-cooperation.

**Where to use:** One high-warmth moment is more persuasive than a stream of medium-warmth moments. Drop a single human anecdote (founder, family member, customer) into one welcome-flow email and never reuse it. Founder voice in DMs reads looser than founder voice in email - slight informality cues real-human presence. Editorial-cadence voice as an in-group signal for the target audience.

**Where NOT to use:** Repeated use of family members as liking anchors cheapens them. Any on-camera or named-personality use must respect each individual's consent and your founder/co-founder visibility policy (some founders deliberately stay off-camera; see `00-foundations/founder-stories.md`).

### 5. Authority

**Canon reference:** Cialdini, _Influence_, Chapter 6 "Authority: Directed Deference".

**One-line definition:** People defer to experts (or symbols of expertise: titles, uniforms, trappings) even when the deference is unwarranted.

**Where to use:** Stack three authorities not one. (1) A category-non-adjacent founder credential the customer can verify (a public track record from a prior industry). (2) A third-party lab or certification report - the trapping-of-authority, the lab's name does the work. (3) A cultural or craft-lineage authority appropriate to the category. Publish the actual lab report PDF or certification on a `/quality` (or equivalent) page - the act of publishing IS the authority.

**Where NOT to use:** Generic authority claims ("award-winning", "voted best by [unspecified]", "trusted by [unspecified] chefs") without naming the magazine, award, or chefs. Reads as filler. Wait until named partners and real awards before deploying these registers.

### 6. Scarcity

**Canon reference:** Cialdini, _Influence_, Chapter 7 "Scarcity: The Rule of the Few".

**One-line definition:** Opportunities seem more valuable when their availability is limited. Strongest when recently introduced, created by competition with others, or emphasises loss rather than gain.

**Where to use:** Shift dominant scarcity mode from time to eligibility. Eligibility-based scarcity ("Code is for new customers only. Yours for 48 hours.") combined with a hard time fence. Eligibility-only scarcity on launch bonuses ("Bonus ships with first orders only - never as a top-up."). Loss-framing on the final urgency email and SMS (per Cialdini ch. 7 loss-framed scarcity outperforms gain-framed by ~2x). Founder-time scarcity as honest lever: replies go straight to the founder until the business outgrows that.

**Where NOT to use:** Banned per `07-anti-patterns/`: "Selling fast", "limited stock", "only X left" - whenever the claim is not literally true at that timestamp. One observed fake-scarcity move can collapse a year of trust building. Real production batch scarcity (a verified count of units in the current run) is acceptable when stated as fact.

### 7. Unity

**Canon reference:** Cialdini, _Pre-Suasion_ (2016), Chapters 11 and 12. Seventh principle, added after _Influence_.

**One-line definition:** We are more persuaded by those we share a "we" identity with - kinship, locality, shared category - than by those we merely like.

**Where to use:** Pick one unity name and use it consistently. The strongest unity names tend to be brand-name portmanteaus (community-identity word built from the brand name plus a suffix like -nauts, -aners, -ites, -ers). The name should be diet/lifestyle-neutral, applicable to every ICP archetype. Wire into specific moments: footer newsletter form, first welcome email subject, order confirmation page, subscription confirmation. Anchor a synchronous shared action (a Sunday ritual, a weekly drop, a monthly recipe) to give the unity name something to actually do (per Cialdini _Pre-Suasion_ ch. 12).

**Where NOT to use:** "Community" - overused, drained of meaning in current DTC. "Members" - reads as supplement-coded in food / lifestyle categories.

## Sutherland's seven moves

[SOURCE: Rory Sutherland, _Alchemy_ (2019) and Spectator columns.]

### 2.1 Context effects

**Canon reference:** Sutherland, _Alchemy_, Chapters 2 and 4.

**One-line definition:** The same product feels premium or budget depending on the context it arrives in - the menu it is listed beside, the typography it is set in, the box it is posted in.

**Where to use:** Unboxing as ritual not logistic. A single sensory line in the post-purchase confirmation email ("Open the box on the counter. The first thing you'll smell is..."). Bonus item on top of the primary product, face-up. Box artwork register matched to the premium-editorial quadrant of your category, not the mass-market quadrant.

**Where NOT to use:** Generic packaging "premium" claims without the context to back them. The premium signal is in the typography, the box weight, the handwritten note - not in the word "premium".

### 2.2 Status signalling at low cost

**Canon reference:** Sutherland, _Alchemy_, throughout.

**One-line definition:** Premium signals do not require premium materials; they require deliberate constraint and visible costly-to-fake markers.

**Where to use:** Treat the unboxing photo as a deliberate signal-generation event. The box itself functions as countertop furniture, not pantry storage. A small ritual-object (a card, magnet, accessory, or display piece) that triggers display behaviour and thereby word-of-mouth.

**Where NOT to use:** Status signals copied from an adjacent but mismatched category (e.g. supplement-register colour blocks and shaker bottles for a food brand). Match the status signals to your actual category register.

### 2.3 Signal-to-cost ratio

**Canon reference:** Sutherland, _Alchemy_, Chapter 3.

**One-line definition:** Costly signals build trust because they cannot be faked cheaply. Brands undervalue these because they are not directly attributable to conversion.

**Where to use:** Published third-party lab tests. Founder personal video DM responses (cost is unscalable founder hours, which is exactly what makes it credible). Printed and posted bonuses, not PDF downloads. Strategic absence from a category-default channel where the brand has chosen not to compete.

**Where NOT to use:** Signals that look costly but can be faked cheaply (generic stock photography, AI-generated testimonials). The cost signals must be operationally real.

### 2.4 Reverse-engineered placebo

**Canon reference:** Sutherland, _Alchemy_, Chapter 8.

**One-line definition:** Placebo is not a trick; it is real value the customer experiences. Brands should deliberately construct rituals that amplify the actual effect of the product.

**Where to use:** Sensory line in the welcome flow describing the actual sensory experience of first use ("The smell when you open the first... is..."). Ritual of opening a fresh unit for each use (vs. a generic container behaviour). Rituals that anchor the product to a specific time, room, or behaviour.

**Where NOT to use:** Body-system or health-outcome placebo framing in categories where regulatory walls forbid medicinal claims. Sensory and craft placebo only. Cross-check against `00-foundations/regulatory-frames.md` before writing.

### 2.5 The Veblen good moment

**Canon reference:** Sutherland in _Alchemy_, Chapter 12.

**One-line definition:** For some goods, raising the price increases demand because the price itself is the value-signal.

**Where to use:** Premium-tier SKUs and gift sets in later phases. A/B test a higher anchor against the assumed-correct price at identical product. Sutherland predicts the higher price often wins on retention and net revenue because the higher price loads a different mental category (premium subscription vs commodity).

**Where NOT to use:** Single-SKU launch at an accessible price point. A launch SKU is usually a premium-everyday good, not a Veblen good. The Veblen move is a phase-two optimisation once the brand has earned the mental category.

### 2.6 Choice architecture beyond decoy

**Canon reference:** Sutherland, _Alchemy_, Chapter 6 and TED talk.

**One-line definition:** Cialdini-style decoys (BEST VALUE / MOST POPULAR / one-time) are the obvious move. Sutherland's wider move: change the choice set entirely, not just the framing within it.

**Where to use:** Phase 2 test - reframe the subscription / one-time binary as a set of meaningfully different commitment lengths matched to the customer's native mental cadence (seasons, quarters, semesters, school years - whatever fits the category and audience). Native framings beat generic "subscribe and save" cadences.

**Where NOT to use:** Replacing standard choice architecture at launch. The default decoy structure works; reframing the choice set is a phase-two optimisation, not a launch-week change.

### 2.7 The dopamine of small reveals

**Canon reference:** Sutherland, _Alchemy_, Chapter 7. Spectator columns on scratch-cards, lotteries, and small dopamine hits.

**One-line definition:** Small, unexpected surprises hit the same neural circuit as gambling wins. Cumulative effect of multiple small reveals is disproportionately retentive.

**Where to use:** A reveal mechanic at the capture popup (scratch-to-reveal, spin-to-win - only if the reward is genuine and the experience matches the brand voice). One unannounced second gift in the first box. A monthly extra in subscription boxes 2, 3, 4. Weekly ritual content with a small reveal element. Vary the founder DM or video monthly.

**Where NOT to use:** Reveals that promise more than they deliver. Sutherland rule: "Always promise less than you deliver, but only by a known small amount."

## Schwartz awareness-stages (the third lens)

[SOURCE: Eugene Schwartz, _Breakthrough Advertising_ (1966).]

Schwartz's five stages: Unaware → Problem-aware → Solution-aware → Product-aware → Most-aware. Copy must match the prospect's current stage.

**Stage-by-stage register:**

- **Stage 1 (Unaware):** Does not know the problem the brand solves exists for them. Register: storytelling, not product. Ad creative usually does this work, not the funnel itself.
- **Stage 2 (Problem-aware):** Knows the problem, has not yet looked for a solution. Register: problem-naming, sensory, lived-experience language. Landers and the second welcome email tend to live here.
- **Stage 3 (Solution-aware):** Knows solutions exist, has not yet chosen one. Register: contrast against the customer's current lived experience, not against another product. The Schwartz-canonical move is to compare "your current life" before-and-after the product, not "our product vs competitor". Implicit category contrasts can be claimed as product features without naming competitors.
- **Stage 4 (Product-aware):** Comparing options inside the category. Register: trust, social proof, guarantee, objection handling.
- **Stage 5 (Most-aware):** Already wants the product, needs a trigger. Register: urgency, scarcity, code, deadline.

**Channel-by-channel mix (rule of thumb for a new premium DTC brand):**

| Channel | Volume share (est.) | Dominant awareness stage |
|---|---|---|
| Organic / SEO | ~15% | Stage 1-2 |
| Direct / brand search | ~10% | Stage 4-5 |
| Paid social (video creative) | ~40% | Stage 2-3 |
| Comment-to-DM / social DM funnel | ~20% | Stage 3-4 |
| Referral / word-of-mouth | ~10% | Stage 3-4 |
| Email (waitlist activation) | ~5% (launch only) | Stage 4 |

Verify the mix against your own ad-spend data once available. The shape, not the exact percentages, is the canonical part.
