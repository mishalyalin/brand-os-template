# Pricing mechanics canon

Owner: Brand voice panel. Status: canonical reference. Version: pricing-mechanics-canon-v1.0.0. Last updated: 2026-05-20.

Compressed reference for five premium-DTC pricing mechanics that determine whether the subscription price holds as a quality signal or erodes into a discount-conditioned commodity. Each principle covers the canonical source, a one-line definition, the funnel stages where it lands hardest, and the places where it should NOT be used.

The school draws on Hermann Simon's _Confessions of the Pricing Man_ (2015), Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke's _Monetizing Innovation_ (2016), the McKinsey / HBR pricing-research corpus, Hilke Plassmann's wine-fMRI work, John Gourville's pennies-a-day mechanism, BCG 2024-2025 pricing-strategy commentary, and the Simon-Kucher Global Pricing Study 2025. Adapt the implementation to your category, price tier, and regulatory walls.

## The thesis in one paragraph

Premium pricing is not commodity pricing. The price the customer pays literally changes how the product is experienced - the neural pleasure signal scales with the price anchor, not just with the product attributes. Holding the price is a brand decision, not a finance decision. The five principles below are the operating discipline that keeps the price anchor intact across the first 18 months of customer compounding.

## The five principles

### 1. Price-sets-taste (the Plassmann neural mechanism)

**Canonical source:** Plassmann, O'Doherty, Shiv and Rangel, "Marketing actions can modulate neural representations of experienced pleasantness", _Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences_ 105:3 (2008) - fMRI study showing that identical wine tasted "better" (measured at the medial orbitofrontal cortex) when labelled at $90 vs $10. The price label literally rewrites the experienced-taste neural signal, not just the verbal report. Replicated in coffee, beer, and chocolate studies 2010-2018. Validated in Hermann Simon, _Confessions of the Pricing Man_ (2015) Chapter 4 - "Price is the strongest perception-shaper marketing has."

**One-line definition:** The price the customer pays literally changes how the product is experienced. The premium-tier product tastes better than the discount-tier version of the same product because the neural pleasure-signal scales with the price-anchor. This is the strongest single argument against under-pricing.

**Where to use:** Hold the locked premium price points without flinching at launch. Do not run launch discounts. Do not run "introducing at low price for the first 100 customers" anchoring tricks that reset the perceived-quality benchmark below the operating price. The first cohort paying full price is more valuable than 3x the cohort paying half price - both for cash and for product-perception conditioning.

**Where NOT to use:** Do not over-extend into price ranges that drop out of the customer's plausible-quality-perception band. Plassmann's mechanism works within the plausibility band; pricing above it produces suspicion, not perceived premium. Pair high price with parallel costly-signal investments (packaging, ingredients, founder-led narrative) so the price reads as justified.

### 2. Per-use anchoring (the Gourville pennies-a-day mechanism)

**Canonical source:** Gourville, "Pandering to Pennies-a-Day: Temporal Reframing in Comparative Advertising", _Journal of Consumer Research_ 24:4 (1998) - reframing an annual cost as a small-denomination per-day cost lifts purchase intent 30-50% when the per-day cost is under the customer's mental "trivial purchase" threshold. The threshold sits at approximately one-half of one currency unit per day for UK / EU / US adult food customers in 2026.

**One-line definition:** The subscription price does not anchor against itself; it anchors against the per-use denominator. Per-use framing that sits inside the trivial-purchase threshold lifts purchase intent on observed conversion. Pick the denominator that matches actual consumption frequency (per-meal, per-application, per-use) - not the dishonest per-day frame if usage is intermittent.

**Where to use:** PDP pricing block sub-headline frames the per-use denominator. Price-objection emails. Founder-led answer to "why so expensive" in customer-service replies. The denominator reinforces the ICP positioning without requiring the math to be re-explained.

**Where NOT to use:** Do not anchor per-day if the product is used less often than daily - that is dishonest and the customer notices. Do not anchor per-unit if the per-unit cost sits below the meaningful-purchase threshold (reads as cheap rather than as value). Do not anchor against another product in your category (Wall-2). The mental-account redirect (see BE-21 in behavioral economics canon) pairs with this principle - your product competes against the mental-account it actually replaces, not the mental-account it sits next to.

### 3. High-to-low tier ordering

**Canonical source:** Suk, Lee and Lichtenstein, "The Effects of Price Presentation on Consumer Purchase Decisions", _Journal of Consumer Research_ 39:2 (2012) - presenting pricing tiers in descending order (highest first) lifts mid-tier conversion 25-60% vs ascending order (lowest first). The effect persists even when consumers are explicitly told about the anchoring mechanism. Validated in Marn and Rosiello, "Managing Price, Gaining Profit", _Harvard Business Review_ 70:5 (1992) - the anchor tier sets the perceived value of every tier below it.

**One-line definition:** Present pricing tiers highest first. The highest tier anchors the middle tier as the value choice. The reverse order (cheapest first) produces the opposite effect - the cheapest tier anchors the next tier up as expensive.

**Where to use:** PDP pricing block ordering: one-time tier (top, anchor) -> subscription tier (middle, target) -> trial tier if applicable (bottom, opt-in for new customers). Pricing-page table column ordering. Subscription-cancellation flow tier-comparison ordering. Email price-comparison ordering in the day-2 onboarding email.

**Where NOT to use:** Do not present the trial tier in isolation on a separate landing page without the anchor tier above. The trial tier in isolation reads as a cheap-product cue and primes the customer to question the value of the subscription tier on the next page.

### 4. Premium promotional discipline

**Canonical source:** Hermann Simon, _Confessions of the Pricing Man_ (2015) Chapter 7 - promotional discounts in premium categories produce a 6-9 month reference-price reset cycle. The post-discount conversion rate drops below the pre-discount baseline because the customer's mental reference-price has shifted downward. Validated in BCG 2024 "Inflation and Pricing" report - 67% of premium brands that ran 2022-2023 discount cycles failed to recover to pre-discount full-price conversion within 12 months. AG1 / Athletic Greens has run zero promotional discounts in 12+ years of public pricing (verified via Wayback Machine 2013-2025).

**One-line definition:** Premium DTC brands do not run promotional discounts. The subscription tier sits below the one-time tier because the customer commits, not because the brand is on sale. Event-based discounts (Black Friday, launch promo, "first month free") erode the reference price and the recovery cycle is 6-9 months long.

**Where to use:** Holiday windows: no discount, founder-led essay instead ("why we don't discount"). Launch day: no founder discount; the trial tier (if you have one) is the structural entry point, not a launch promo. Customer-service replies to "is there a code": "subscription is the discount" - founder-voice answer, not deflection.

**Where NOT to use:** The trial tier (if it exists) is the one exception - it is a structural trial price, not an event-based discount, and it pairs with conversion mechanics (trial-to-subscription flow) rather than urgency mechanics. If the trial price ever drifts into a "limited time" frame, it converts from structural pricing into event pricing and breaks the discipline.

### 5. Sustainability premium headroom

**Canonical source:** BCG 2025 "Luxury Sustainability Premium" report - consumers in DTC food + beverage + supplement categories pay an average 9.7% premium for verified-sustainable products vs equivalent non-sustainable products, on observed cross-category survey data (n=12,000 UK + EU + US adults). The premium is contingent on (a) the sustainability claim being verifiable (third-party cert or visible production decision), (b) the brand making the claim prominently rather than as a footer disclosure, (c) the customer being able to articulate the sustainability decision to a friend.

**One-line definition:** A verified-sustainable production decision creates approximately 9.7% pricing headroom on the operating price tier. The premium does not require a price increase to capture - it requires the existing price to be felt as well-earned because the customer can name the sustainability decision behind it.

**Where to use:** Founder-story page section on the specific verifiable sustainability decision (recyclable packaging, third-party certification, ingredient sourcing). Day-14 founder-video email subject line. Recipe-card insert footer naming the decision. Pricing-page sub-headline tying the price to the costly signal. The premium does not require a price increase - it requires the existing price to be felt as well-earned.

**Where NOT to use:** Do not lead the lander hero with sustainability. The Layer 0 ICP gate is the brand promise; sustainability is the costly-signal layer beneath the promise. Sustainability copy at hero position dilutes the brand promise. Sustainability lives in the founder story, in mid-lifecycle emails, and in the unboxing experience - not in the first 100vh of the lander.

## Flags that surface during canon application

When applying these mechanics to a real brand's pricing architecture, two findings recur as [CONTRADICTS-EXISTING] flags worth A/B testing rather than treating as doctrine calls:

- **Charm endings (X.99) vs round prices (X.00) in premium positioning.** Troll, Hauck et al meta-analysis 2024 (38 studies, n=15,000 consumers) - charm endings lift conversion in commodity categories by 5-12pp but in luxury / premium categories round prices outperform charm-endings by 3-7pp on observed willingness-to-pay. The Plassmann mechanism (principle 1 above) is part of the explanation. Recommended action: A/B test charm vs round at the subscription tier 6 months post-launch; let data decide.

- **Rule of 100 (discount-framing format).** Chen, Marmorstein, Tsiros and Rao, "When More Is Less", _Journal of Marketing_ 76:4 (2012) - under 100 currency units frame as percentage ("save 20%"), over 100 frame as absolute amount ("save $X"). The framing that loads less mental arithmetic wins on conversion by 8-12pp. The "save 2 months" frame for annual prepay (subscription-mechanics principle 3) sits in the over-100 territory and is technically non-conformant with the Rule of 100. Default to "save 2 months" per Patrick Campbell's empirical lift evidence; A/B test against "save X" framing post-launch.
